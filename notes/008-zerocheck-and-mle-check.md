# Lesson 008 — Every constraint must vanish

2026-10-23 · Sumcheck variants · Introduced; understanding unchecked.

## Why zero sum is insufficient

Let e(u) be the error in the constraint at Boolean index u. Correctness requires e(u)=0 for every u. In characteristic two, errors [1,1,0,0] have sum zero even though two constraints fail: 1+1=0. An ordinary unweighted zero-sum claim loses the needed information.

## Evaluate the multilinear extension of the error table

Bind the witness/error table before the verifier samples a uniform point τ in Kⁿ. Define

\[
E(\tau)=\sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^n}e(u)\operatorname{eq}_n(\tau,u).
\]

This is the error table's multilinear extension. If all errors vanish, E is identically zero. If any error is nonzero, E is a nonzero multilinear polynomial by uniqueness of interpolation. Its total degree is at most n, so

\[
\Pr_{\tau\gets K^n}[E(\tau)=0]\leq\min(1,n/|K|).
\]

This is a multivariate root bound, not a statement that the 2ⁿ weights are mutually independent random scalars. They are products generated by n independent field challenges.

One way to see the bound is induction: write E=A+τ_last B. If B is nonzero, it vanishes at the preceding coordinates with probability at most (n−1)/|K|; otherwise the last coordinate has at most one root. If B is identically zero, apply the bound to A in fewer variables. The bound is loose when actual degree is smaller or the field is tiny.

For the error table [1,1,0,0], indexed x+2y, E(τ₀,τ₁)=1−τ₁. At τ₁=α in F₄ it is 1+α, which is nonzero. This table slips through only when τ₁=1, probability 1/|K|. By contrast the unweighted sum is always zero.

The remaining task is to prove the claimed weighted sum equals zero without the verifier reading every error. This is where sumcheck/MLE-check enters.

## The error table and the constraint polynomial differ

Suppose each Boolean constraint error is given by e(u)=g(u), where g is a polynomial composition of witness multilinears. Then E is the multilinear extension of g's *Boolean values*. Generally E is not the same formal polynomial as g.

Example: g(X)=X²+X. At both bits it is zero, so its error table is [0,0] and E is identically zero. Nevertheless g(α)=1 in F₄. Rejecting merely because g is nonzero at a random extension-field point would reject this valid vanishing-on-bits relation.

This is why zerocheck targets a weighted sum over Boolean constraints, not the assertion that the original constraint polynomial is identically zero as a formal polynomial.

## Ordinary sumcheck versus factored MLE-check

An ordinary sumcheck can handle

\[
0\stackrel?=\sum_u\operatorname{eq}_n(\tau,u)g(u).
\]

If g has individual degree at most d, the product with eq has individual degree at most d+1. Binius also uses a specialized MLE-check that factors the known equality weights out of the sent round polynomial.

At a round binding coordinate k, with higher coordinates already fixed to r, the prover sends

\[
R_k(T)=\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^k}
\operatorname{eq}_k(\tau_{<k},v)\,g(v,T,r_{>k}).
\]

The current weighted claim c must satisfy

\[
c=(1-\tau_k)R_k(0)+\tau_kR_k(1).
\]

Then the verifier samples a fresh r_k and updates c to R_k(r_k). Notice the two different roles: τ specifies the weighted-sum/MLE claim; r is the fresh sequence used to check it. The sent polynomial has degree at most d, saving the extra degree from the known equality factor.

At the end, the output is g(r)=c. It need not be zero, even when every Boolean constraint is satisfied. In the one-variable g(X)=X²+X example, an honest initial claim is zero, R(T)=T²+T passes the weighted endpoint check for every τ, and challenge r=α leads to final claimed value 1. The terminal check authenticates that 1 equals g(α); it does not compare it with the initial zero claim.

No division by eq(τ,r) is needed in this factored formulation. The endpoint weights are handled directly, so zero equality factors are not an obstacle to the round update.

## Exact code detail

At pinned revision `4428e759dd27956bd5b9ae5025d67eb8ccc1accf`, `crates/ip/src/mlecheck.rs::verify` iterates over τ coordinates in reverse order, receives degree-many coefficients, samples a challenge, recovers the round polynomial, evaluates it, and returns a terminal claim plus challenges in sampling order.

Unlike ordinary sumcheck, `mlecheck::RoundProof` omits the **constant coefficient**. For R(T)=a₀+…+a_d T^d, weighted endpoint consistency gives

\[
c=a_0+\tau_k\sum_{j=1}^d a_j,
\qquad a_0=c-\tau_k\sum_{j=1}^d a_j.
\]

The omitted constant can therefore be reconstructed without division, including τ_k=0 or 1. Ordinary sumcheck instead omits the highest coefficient, as covered in Lesson 007.

Only plain verification and coefficient recovery were analyzed here. The same file's zero-knowledge masking routines are a separate topic; reading their presence does not establish the full ZK construction.

## Soundness and implementation scope

Zerocheck's initial random-MLE test contributes at most n/|K| additional error. Factored MLE-check contributes at most nd/|K| under the analogous fixed-polynomial, degree, fresh-challenge, and authenticated-terminal-value assumptions. A simple combined bound is n(d+1)/|K|, capped by 1; concrete Binius reductions and batching require their own accounting.

The generic method here is a building block, not a claim that every Binius64 constraint path uses this exact routine. Its BitAnd protocol has specialized reductions and optimizations to study separately. Witness binding, transcript security, and commitment openings also remain necessary.

Recall: if a constraint polynomial vanishes at all Boolean inputs, must its value at a random extension-field point be zero? No; g(X)=X²+X is the counterexample.
